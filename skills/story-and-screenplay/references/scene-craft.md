# Scene craft

Paraphrased from common scene-construction teaching (goal, conflict, turn, value shift, subtext). Use with the step 2 and step 3 protocol in `../SKILL.md`.

## 1. The scene engine
Every scene answers five questions in one line each:
1. Whose scene is it (the point-of-view character)?
2. Goal: what do they want right now, in this room, in this minute?
3. Conflict: who or what resists?
4. Turn: the moment the situation changes direction (a revelation, a refusal, a gift, an interruption).
5. Value shift: which life value moves (trust, hope, power, love, safety, pride) and in which direction, plus to minus or minus to plus.
If the value does not move, cut the scene or merge it. A scene that only gives information is a delivery; hide the information inside conflict.

## 2. Enter late, leave early
- Start at the last moment before the change, not at the arrival. No walking in, no greetings unless the greeting is the conflict.
- End on the turn or one beat after it. Cut away before the characters explain what just happened.
- In 5 to 15 s clips this is decisive: the first frame should already be mid-situation.

## 3. Subtext
- People rarely say what they mean when it is risky. Text is what is said; subtext is what is wanted or feared.
- Method: write the line the character wants to say, then write a different line they can safely say (about money, food, weather, work) that carries it. The audience reads the gap.
- Carry subtext through objects and actions: a mother serving the favourite dish after a quarrel, a hand pulled back from a handshake, a phone turned face down.
- Contradiction: let body and words disagree (smiling while refusing, saying "I am fine" while gripping the rail).

## 4. Scene types
| Type | Core move | Craft notes |
|---|---|---|
| Reveal | A hidden fact enters the room | Plant a question earlier; let a prop or a look deliver it; show the reaction shot longer than the fact |
| Confrontation | Two wants collide, power shifts | Escalate in steps; each speech raises the cost; end with someone choosing silence or action |
| Romance | Attraction against a barrier (shyness, class, caste, family, timing) | Use small business (a glance, a dropped item, shared shelter); never say "I love you" early; touch is the climax of the scene |
| Comedy | Character logic pushed past reason | Build a rule, then break it; the straight person reacts; timing lives in cuts and pauses |
| Action | Goal under physical threat | One clear geography, one clear objective, a reversal every few seconds, a cost (injury, loss) |
| Emotional climax | The choice that proves the arc | Fewer words, held close-ups, one prop callback, music or silence deliberately |
| Entrance (hero or heroine) | The audience learns who they are in one image | Show competence, charisma or vulnerability by action; see `indian-cinema-storytelling.md` |

## 5. Pacing
- Rhythm alternates: tension then release, loud then quiet, wide then close. Two high-intensity scenes back to back numb the viewer.
- Count beats, not words: a beat is one change in the exchange. A 20 s scene holds 2 to 3 beats; a 60 s scene holds 5 to 7.
- Shorten dialogue first, then action, then description. A line over 12 words for a 5 s clip will not fit.
- Silence is a tool: a pause before the key line often gives more than the line.
- Escalate: the second obstacle is harder or more personal than the first.

## 6. Visual storytelling checklist (show, do not tell)
Tick each before locking the scene.
1. Can the scene be understood with the sound off?
2. Is the main emotion visible in a face, a posture, a prop or an action, before a word is spoken?
3. Is there one signature image (the frame someone would remember)?
4. Is the setting doing story work (a cracked wall, a half-eaten meal, a festival lamp, wet footwear)?
5. Is there a prop with meaning that can return later?
6. Does light or colour carry the value shift (cold to warm, shadow to sun)?
7. Is each shot a single action a generator can render (no "remembers", "realises", "decides" as bare verbs)? Convert them to behaviour: "realises" becomes "stops mid-step, eyes drop to the letter".
8. Are exposition lines replaced with behaviour or a prop?
9. Is every character distinguishable by silhouette and colour at phone size?
10. Does the last image differ from the first in meaning?

## 7. Conversion table: thought to behaviour
| Thought or feeling | Filmable behaviour |
|---|---|
| She is nervous | Thumbnail picks at the bangle, breath held, steps back half a step |
| He is lying | Eyes go to the left, a too-quick smile, hand covers the mouth |
| They are in love | Both look away at the same time, one hides a smile |
| He is proud | Chest lifts, chin level, ordinary task done with care |
| Grief | Sets two cups, then returns one; sits in the other's chair |
| Authority | Others pause when he enters; he does not look up |

## 8. AI-readiness reminders
- One location per sequence, one light state, one time of day.
- No more than two speaking characters in a single generated shot; cut between them instead.
- Crowds and complex hand business are fragile: give them to wide shots or inserts.
- Avoid text on screen, signage with exact words, and mirrors in key shots.
- Continuity: costume, prop in hand and time of day are written into every scene header or shot row so the prompt-engineer repeats them.
