# Story structure

Craft conventions, paraphrased from widely taught models (McKee, Syd Field, Snyder, the sequence approach, Pixar's story rules). They are tools for diagnosis, not laws. The full 15-beat table and long-form workflow live in `../../film-studio/references/story-and-script.md`; this file is the compact working set.

## 1. Premise and logline
- Premise: one sentence, who wants what, what stops them. Test: a stranger repeats it back correctly.
- Logline formula (25 to 35 words): "When [inciting event], a [flawed protagonist] must [goal] before [stakes], but [opposing force]." Irony helps: the thing they want is exactly what their flaw makes hard.
- For an ad, the logline is one line: "[Person] is [stuck in a small problem] until [product moment], and feels [emotion]."
- Pixar-style spine (paraphrased): "Once upon a time... every day... until one day... because of that... because of that... until finally..." Use it to find the chain of cause and effect; if a step is "and then", the story is a list, not a chain.

## 2. Character: want, need, flaw, arc
| Part | Meaning | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Want | Outer goal the character chases | Can be filmed as an action |
| Need | Inner lesson or truth they lack | Often opposed to the want |
| Flaw | Habit or belief that blocks the need | Shows in behaviour in the first minute |
| Arc | Change from flaw to growth (or fall) | Visible in a final choice, not a speech |
- A character is defined by choices under pressure, not by description. Give each lead one contradiction (gentle but proud, funny but lonely).
- Antagonist: the force that makes the want hard. It can be a person, a custom, money, time, or the self.
- Small cast for AI video: 1 to 3 recurring characters, each visually distinct (age, build, clothing colour) so faces do not blur together.

## 3. Three-act
| Act | Share | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Set-up | about 25 percent | Normal world, flaw, inciting incident, decision to act |
| 2 Confrontation | about 50 percent | Rising obstacles, midpoint reversal (about 50 percent), all is lost (about 75 percent) |
| 3 Resolution | about 25 percent | Final choice, climax, new normal |
- Turning points flip the direction of the story; they are caused by a choice, not by luck.
- Theme is the argument the story makes about how to live. State it as a tension ("loyalty versus ambition"), prove it through the ending's choice.

## 4. Beat sheet (compact, percent of runtime)
Opening image 0 to 2 | Set-up and theme hint 2 to 10 | Catalyst about 10 | Debate 10 to 25 | Break into Act 2 about 25 | Fun and promise 25 to 50 | Midpoint about 50 | Pressure rises 50 to 75 | All is lost about 75 | Dark moment 75 to 80 | Break into Act 3 about 80 | Climax 80 to 95 | Final image 95 to 100. The final image mirrors the opening image with a changed meaning.

## 5. Sequences
Eight sequences of 10 to 15 minutes in a feature, each a mini-movie with a want, obstacle and partial resolution that hands a new problem to the next. Mapping: seq 1 set-up, seq 2 catalyst to Act 2, seqs 3 and 4 to the midpoint, seqs 5 and 6 to all is lost, seq 7 the climax run-up, seq 8 climax and resolution. For shorts, use 3 to 5 sequences of one location each.

## 6. Setups and payoffs
- Plant early, pay late: every prop, line or habit shown in the first third should return changed. Introduce the object innocently, return it with new meaning (the torn letter, the cracked bangle, the unfinished stitch).
- Chekhov check: if a gun is shown, it fires; if it fires, it was shown. Cut orphans.
- Callback lines: repeat a line with a changed context for the emotional payoff.
- Value shift ledger: list each scene's start and end value (plus to minus or reverse). Two scenes in a row with the same shift are one scene.

## 7. Short-film structure (3 to 10 minutes)
1. Hook image (0 to 10 s): a question the viewer must answer.
2. Situation and flaw (to 20 percent).
3. One inciting disturbance (about 20 percent).
4. Two escalations, each worse or more intimate (to 70 percent).
5. One irreversible choice (about 75 percent).
6. Climax in one location, one action (to 90 percent).
7. Final image with a changed meaning of the hook (last 10 percent).
One protagonist, one want, one location per sequence, one ending image. Cut any subplot.

## 8. Ad structure (6, 15, 30 seconds)
Hooks and splits are rules of thumb from creator and agency guidance, not tested findings; measure on the channel.
| Length | Arc | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| 6 s | Hook and payoff only: a pattern-interrupt image, then product or message with one beat of feeling | Hook 0 to 2, payoff 2 to 6 |
| 15 s | Hook, build, payoff | Hook 0 to 2 (not a logo), stakes 2 to 6 or 2 to 11, payoff to the end with one brand moment |
| 30 s | Problem, agitate, solution, ask | Problem 0 to 3, cost 3 to 8, solution 8 to 25, close 25 to 30 |
- Pick one hook type: result, mistake, tension, contrast or question. Write the payoff first, then the hook.
- One idea, one product, one ask. The story is the emotion before and after, not the product list.
- Voiceover pace for a 30 s spot is roughly 65 to 80 words in English; Tamil and Hindi run longer, so cut harder.
- Mid-length (45 to 60 s): stacked micro-payoffs every 8 to 10 s rather than one payoff at the end.
- Skincare and cosmetics: the payoff is a feeling or ritual (calm, care, confidence), never a medical or guaranteed result; no before-and-after shown as real; claims go to the qualified team first.
- Mobile framing: 9:16, faces large, key action inside safe zones (14 percent top, 35 percent bottom, 6 percent sides).

## 9. Theme check before writing
State: Want, Need, Flaw, final choice, final image, one-line theme. If the final choice does not answer the theme tension, rewrite the climax before the dialogue.
