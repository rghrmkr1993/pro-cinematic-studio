# Vocal craft (voice types, registers, vibrato versus gamaka, diction, technique vocabulary)

Extends `voice-studio/SKILL.md` and `theory-and-tune.md` section 4; read them first. Labels: F sourced in session research 2026-10-14 (`sources.md` section "2026-10-14 additions"), E pedagogy craft knowledge, U unverified. Ranges are typical, individuals vary.

HARD RULE: never prompt an AI to imitate a named singer's voice, and never clone one (no sound-alike, no fine-tune on a real singer without written consent and licence). Use the technique vocabulary below. Final Tamil vocals are human (field test 2026-10-13, `ai-music-prompting.md` section 8).

## 1. Voice types and ranges (scientific pitch, E)
| Type | Typical range | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| Bass | E2 to E4 | deep, dark, authoritative |
| Baritone | A2 to A4 | warm, strong, versatile |
| Tenor | C3 to C5 (A4 comfortable) | bright, heroic, romantic |
| Alto (contralto) | F3 to F5 | rich, dark, grounded |
| Mezzo-soprano | A3 to A5 | warm, flexible |
| Soprano | C4 to C6 | bright, light, soaring |
- Classification relies on range used in performance (centre of the working range), timbre, and where the passaggio sits, more than on the extreme notes (F, Johnson and Kempster 2011 as summarised on voicescience.org).
- Indian vocabulary (E): low octave mandra sthayi, middle madhya sthayi, high tara sthayi. Singers speak of sruti (kattai) rather than fach; film usage divides "male voice" and "female voice" with a wide spread, and many male playback voices are light baritone-tenors (E, U). Carnatic training uses three octaves from lower Pa to upper Pa (E). Kattai is covered in `carnatic-deep.md` section 2.

## 2. Male and female: ranges, formants, registers
- Pitch: average male speaking fundamental about 100 to 130 Hz, female about 180 to 230 Hz (E). Females are roughly an octave above males on the same melody.
- Formants: vocal-tract resonances (F1 to F5) shape vowels and timbre; shorter female tracts give higher formants (F). Vowel shaping is the main tool for tone.
- Singer's formant: a spectral peak near 2.5 to 3.5 kHz formed by clustering F3 to F5 through a narrowed epilarynx and a wide pharynx; it lets classical voices carry over an orchestra. Mean values by type: soprano about 3.1 kHz, tenor about 2.7, baritone about 2.45, bass about 2.4; sopranos can struggle to use it high because the harmonics are too sparse (F, Nov 2022 PMC study as reported). Pop and film singers use microphones and need less of it, but a "ring" at 2 to 4 kHz still gives clarity (E).
- Registers: chest voice (low, thick vocal-fold vibration, full and speaking-like), head voice (high, thin, light), mixed voice (blend, smooth through the passaggio), falsetto (breathy, flute-like, little fold contact) (E). Passaggio: the register transition; trained singers lower F1 and F2 by lengthening the tract near the passaggio while keeping the 3 kHz cluster (F).
- Whistle and belt: belt is chest-dominant loud mix; whistle is the very top of the female range (E).
- Carnatic practice: no head-chest "break" ideal; a continuous resonant voice through the three sthayis, with the singer choosing Sa so the middle is the speaking zone (E).

## 3. Vibrato versus gamaka (different things)
- Western vibrato: periodic pitch oscillation, typically about 5 to 7 Hz, extent about a semitone peak-to-peak in trained classical singers (F; ranges in sources are 4.5 to 6.5 Hz for trained singers; below 4 Hz reads as a wobble, above 7 Hz as a bleat); added to sustained notes as a steady shimmer (F, E).
- Gamaka: shaped movements, slides and oscillations on and between notes, raga-specific, with variable speed and width; the oscillation of a kampita is one kind and is not a uniform vibrato (F). It forms the grammar of the raga, not decoration (E).
- Test: if every long note carries the same sine-shaped wobble, it is Western vibrato; if the note bends toward a neighbour with a shape that fits the raga and changes with context, it is gamaka.
- Prompting: Western: "gentle even vibrato on sustained notes". Carnatic: "raga-shaped gamaka, slides and oscillations, no uniform vibrato, straight tone only on the shruti-aligned held note".
- Modern pop and film: straighter tone with late, light vibrato, plus occasional gamaka-like glides (E).

## 4. Breath, resonance, nasality and timbre words
- Breath control: low (diaphragmatic) breath support, rib expansion, steady airflow; long phrases without audible gasp; breath support fails first under studio fatigue (E, from a course article). Breath placement is part of phrasing; silent catch-breaths at phrase edges; in slow lines a breathy onset can add intimacy.
- Resonance: chest (warmth), mouth and mask (forward, "ring"), head (lightness); bringing sound forward raises brightness (E).
- Nasality: nasal consonants (m, n, ng) help carry sound in Tamil singing; heavy twang or nasal tone is used in some folk and gaana styles; avoid in lullabies (E).
- Timbre vocabulary for prompts: bright (forward, high formants), dark (low formants, round), velvety (smooth, low-mid warmth), husky (breathy edge, grit), airy (breathy), open-throated (relaxed, resonant), nasal (twangy, forward), thin or reedy, creamy, chesty, steely (strong ring), silvery (light, pure), warm, intimate (close-mic, soft), gritty (distorted edge), clean, edgy, smoky. Add "natural, close-mic, slight imperfect timing, audible breaths" for human feel.

## 5. High versus low pitch: emotion (E)
- Low: grave, intimate, authoritative, calm, sorrowful, sensual; whisper-lows for lullabies and confession.
- Middle: conversational, honest, warm, safe for narration.
- High: excitement, joy, innocence, urgency, longing, ecstasy; very high and strained: anguish, shriek, climax.
- Rising line: hope, tension; falling line: resolution, sorrow, sleep; wide leaps: yearning; narrow steps: intimacy.
- Dynamics: soft = intimacy, loud = triumph or protest; a sudden hush after a loud line gives shock.

## 6. Singing patterns
- Melisma: one syllable over many notes; used heavily in Carnatic and in Tamil film for emotion; test it with a vowel, not a consonant (E).
- Ornamentation: gamaka, turns, trills (murki: quick ornament of 3 or 4 notes, hindustani-influenced), grace notes, mordents, glides (E).
- Sangati: progressive variation of the same line, each repeat slightly more elaborate, building emotion; a hallmark of Carnatic kriti and carried into Tamil film songs (F, E, U on school-specific definitions).
- Sustained notes: pitch locked to the shruti drone, with natural swell and fade; a clean, steady tone ends phrases and creates a sense of space.
- Glides (jaru): slow bend between notes; fast flicks (orikai) at ends; portamento is similar in Western singing.
- Phrase architecture: pickup, peak, resolution; breath at the natural comma; put the peak on a long open vowel.
- Call and response: lead singer and chorus (gaana, kuthu, villupattu).

## 7. Tamil diction for singing (E, with F where noted)
- Vowel length is phonemic: short (kuril) 1 unit, long (nedil) about 2 units; diphthongs about 1.5 (F). Hold nedil vowels clearly longer than kuril; in a lilting line this fixes the meter.
- Retroflex consonants: ட (ṭ), ண (ṇ), ள (ḷ), ழ (zh), plus ற/ன distinctions; curl the tongue tip back and avoid dental softening (E, F on zha).
- The zha sound ழ is a retroflex approximant (IPA ɻ): tongue tip points back toward the palate without touching, air flows over the centre; ள is a lateral retroflex (ɭ) where the tip touches. Colloquial dialects shift ழ to ɭ (south) or j (north); standard singing keeps ɻ (F). A recent benchmark found AI TTS systems collapse zha and lose vowel-length contrast, so human review is required (F, 3P).
- Vowel shaping: five pure vowels (a, i, u, e, o), long and short; keep them pure, do not diphthongise in held notes; open vowels (aa, o) sustain best; finish on a nasal or long vowel rather than a hard stop (E).
- Consonant clusters and gemination (doubled consonants like ட்ட, த்த) need a small hold before release; use short, light consonants on fast lines and sharper ones for rhythmic effect.
- Stress: Tamil is syllable-timed; place the beat on the syllable, not on a stress; spoken-style (colloquial) Tamil sings more naturally than formal literary Tamil in pop (see `theory-and-tune.md` section 4).
- Aspirate loanwords (kha, gha, bha) are used in Sanskritised lyrics; keep them clean or avoid.
- Always: native singer or coach; phonetic Latin line in the brief; record the pronunciation guide.

## 8. Learn from the greats: technique traits (era archetypes, descriptive only)
Public profiles and course write-ups describe these traits; no names are needed and none go in prompts (F where noted, otherwise E).
- Golden-era Carnatic concert voices: deep shruti discipline, consistent tonal centre, rich gamaka grammar, devotional gravity; long unbroken lines; microtonal precision (E).
- A celebrated classical-trained film voice (female): purity of tone, tight microtonal intonation, graceful control of gamaka and ornament, sustained lines without audible strain, careful phrasing that adapted as recording styles changed (F, one journalistic profile; U on generalisation).
- Legendary male playback voice (descriptive archetype): effortless breath, conversational ease, emotive phrasing, smooth movement between chest and mixed registers, subtle ornament that serves the lyric; the sense of "talking the song" (E).
- Tamil film voices of the 1950s to 60s: clear diction, light vibrato, restrained ornament, melody-first singing, suited to unison strings (E).
- 1970s to 80s: more gamaka-aware melodic lines over orchestral and folk textures; warmth and long breath on slow numbers (E).
- 1990s: crisp studio-polished tone, tighter timing, layered doubles, close-mic intimacy, rhythmic vocals (E).
- 2000s onward: wider stylistic range: soft breathy lines, rap and gaana grit, vocal fry, spoken-sung hybrids; heavy use of doubling and pitch correction in pop (E).
- Folk and gaana: raw, chesty, call-and-response, rhythmic phrasing, humour and pathos in one breath, speech-near melody (E).
- Training habits common to many (F, course sources): sruti-based practice with tambura, sarali and alankara exercises, slow practice of gamakas, breath exercises, recording self-review (E).
- Take-away for briefs: ask a human singer for "effortless breath, conversational phrasing, restrained ornament" (or "classical gamaka grammar, pure tone") rather than naming anyone.
