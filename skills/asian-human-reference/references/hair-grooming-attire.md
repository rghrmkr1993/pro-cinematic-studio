# Hair, grooming and attire

Labels: [E] estimate from general cultural knowledge, [V] verified in cited source, [U] unverified. Everything is a RANGE. Choose attire from ONE coherent tradition per outfit, name the garment precisely, and give a scene reason for any religious or ceremonial item.

## Hairstyles

### Women and girls
- Long oiled hair in a single or double braid (South, East, West India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka); school girls often two tight braids with ribbons [E].
- Low bun or chignon, often with jasmine (mallipoo) or gajra (flower string) on festive days, mostly South India; wedding hair with layered flowers [E]. Flowers wilt and sit slightly uneven; do not render as a perfect crown.
- Loose waves, side parting, hair clips and scrunchies in urban settings; blow-dried layers, balayage, burgundy or brown henna tones.
- Short bob, pixie, shaved sides, natural greys: normal in cities.
- Older women: grey or white hair, low knot, thinning at the parting; widows in some communities wear plain styles, but do not default every elder woman to this.
- East Asia: low ponytail, straight bob with blunt fringe, long straight hair, perm waves; Korea and Japan have wide dye and styling culture; Chinese older women often short permed hair.
- Southeast Asia: long straight hair, buns, hijab styles with inner cap (Malaysia, Indonesia, southern Thailand, Mindanao) worn by choice; Filipino urban styles widely dyed.

### Men and boys
- Short back and sides with side parting, oiled and combed (common in older men), fade cuts, textured crops, longer wavy hair, man buns in urban youth.
- Receding hairlines, shaved heads (including after temple tonsure or mourning, state the reason), comb-overs.
- Sikh men: many keep uncut hair (kesh) in a top knot (joora) under a turban (dastar) or a patka (small cloth for boys and some men); many Sikhs do not wear turbans or cut hair [V in general, see sources].
- Other turbans and head cloths: Rajasthani pagri (varied colours and tie by caste, region), Maharashtrian pheta, Gandhi cap (topi), kufi or skullcap among some Muslim men, Kashmiri karakul cap. Match to a scene reason.
- East Asia: short neat cuts, undercuts, two-block, soft bowl cuts in Korea and Japan fashion; Chinese elderly men often flat caps.
- Southeast Asia: short cuts, songkok or peci (Malay and Indonesian cap) on formal and religious occasions.

### Children
Tidy oiled side partings, bob cuts, two braids, topknot on young boys for rites, buzz cuts for hot climates; messy play hair is normal.

## Beards and moustaches
- India: moustaches are common across ages (thick handlebar in Rajasthan and the north; trimmed in cities; the Kerala mundu-and-moustache look); stubble, designer beards, long beards for some Sikh and Muslim men, grey beards on elders. Clean-shaven is equally common.
- Many East Asian men have sparse facial hair; a stubble or thin goatee exists but heavy full beards are rarer [E]. Do not force a full beard by default.
- Southeast Asia: light to moderate facial hair; Indian and Malay men may have fuller beards.
- Women: do not render hairless faces by default; fine vellus hair and sometimes mild moustache hair are normal.

## Marks and ornaments
Use sparingly, with scene reason, and state absence when not wanted.

- **Bindi**: a dot or shape on the forehead, worn mostly by Hindu women (and some others as fashion); size, colour and shape vary (red, maroon, black, stick-on gem). Many women, including Hindus, do not wear one daily. Not worn by Muslim, most Christian or Sikh women as a religious mark [E], though some wear it as fashion. Do NOT put bindis on men, children by default, East Asian or Southeast Asian characters, or as a generic "Indian" tag. A tilak or vermilion (kumkum) mark on a man or boy is a separate temple or prayer mark.
- **Sindoor**: red powder in the hair parting, worn by some married Hindu women in many regions; many married women do not wear it. Do not add it to young or unmarried characters.
- **Mangalsutra, toe rings, bangles** (glass in Maharashtra and the north, gold in the south, shakha-pola in Bengal): marital markers in some communities; again optional.
- **Mehendi (henna)**: on hands and feet for weddings, Eid, Karwa Chauth, Teej; designs differ (Rajasthani, Arabic, Indo-Arabic); mehendi fades from orange to brown in days.
- **Kajal**: eye line in the waterline or lower lash line; used by women, children, and some men; not a thick winged Western liner by default.
- **Nose rings**: nath (large, bridal), nose stud (laung), ring (naths in Maharashtra); left nostril is common for women in India [E]; they are worn by Indian women of many faiths, and by some men as fashion. Ear piercings: multiple, for all genders in many communities.
- **Rudraksha, sacred thread (janeu), tulsi mala, kada (Sikh steel bangle), kirpan, kara, kangha**: religious items. Do not use as costume decoration; the five Sikh Ks (kesh, kangha, kara, kirpan, kachera) are practised by Khalsa Sikhs, not all Sikhs [V general].
- **Hijab, burqa, niqab, tudung, dupatta over head**: worn by choice by many Muslim women and some others; do not use as a shorthand for oppression or for all Muslim characters; in India many Muslim women do not cover.
- East Asia: very little religious iconography in everyday life; Buddhist prayer beads (mala/juzu), amulets, small jade pendants; Korean hanbok accessories. Southeast Asia: Thai amulet necklaces, Buddhist sai sin white threads on wrists, Balinese offerings (canang sari) and headbands for ritual.

## Everyday attire

### India
- Women: salwar kameez or kurta with leggings, jeans with kurti, saree (cotton for daily, silk for events; draped Nivi style in many places; Kerala set mundu; Bengali atpoure; Gujarati seedha pallu; Maharashtrian nauvari 9-yard), uniforms for work (nurse, bank), western wear, dupatta carried or pinned.
- Men: shirt and trousers, kurta-pyjama, dhoti or veshti (Tamil), mundu (Kerala), lungi (south and east, informal), lungi at home, jeans and t-shirt, sherwani for weddings, pathani suit, bandhgala jacket, Nehru jacket, Gandhi topi, safas, school uniforms.
- Children: school uniform with tie and shoes, frocks, kurta sets.
- Footwear: chappals, kolhapuris, mojaris (festive), sneakers; shoes are removed before entering homes and temples.

### East Asia
- Everyday modern clothing: jeans, tees, blazers, school uniforms (seifuku in Japan, school uniforms in Korea and China, sometimes track suits in China), office wear. Traditional dress is festive: kimono and yukata (Japan, left side crosses over right for the living), hanbok (Korea), qipao or cheongsam and Tang suit (China), Hanfu (a modern revival, often worn as a styled outfit).
- Do not use traditional dress for everyday "average" scenes.

### Southeast Asia
- Thailand: modern casual and uniforms; chut thai for ceremonies; Vietnam: ao dai for school and formal occasions, ao ba ba rural; Philippines: barong tagalog (formal men), terno or baro't saya; Indonesia: batik shirts for formal office, kebaya, sarong, songkok; Malaysia: baju kurung, baju melayu, samping; Singapore: smart casual and uniforms.

## Festive and ceremonial attire (examples)
- Weddings vary by faith and region: Hindu north (red lehenga, sherwani, safa), Hindu south (Kanchipuram silk saree, veshti, gold), Bengali (red-white saree, topor), Muslim nikah (white or pastel, dupatta), Sikh anand karaj (pastels, kirpan on groom), Christian (white gown or saree), Parsi (sari with embroidered border). Name the specific tradition or choose "mixed urban wedding".
- Festivals: Diwali, Pongal, Onam (off-white and gold kasavu), Durga Puja (red-border white saree, dhunuchi), Eid (kurtas, sharara), Holi (white clothes with colour powder), Baisakhi; Songkran (flower shirts), Lunar New Year (red clothes), Tet (ao dai), Hari Raya (baju melayu and kurung), Obon (yukata), Chuseok (hanbok).

## Respect rules
- Never place a religious symbol on a person just to signal ethnicity.
- Do not mix mourning white (Hindu), bridal white (Christian), and Chinese funeral white and black unless the scene requires it.
- Do not put sacred figures, deities, or texts in prompts as props or ads; avoid depicting deities on shoes, bikinis, or food packs.
- Feet and shoes: avoid pointing soles or putting shoes near altars in video.
- Do not show a turban being removed or mocked; do not use a turban as costume for a non-Sikh character without context.
- Left hand for eating or giving is a convention to follow when showing traditional settings (see body-language file).
- Avoid dressing one character in items from different faiths without a reason (bindi plus hijab plus cross).
- Keep clothing coherent: matching saree and blouse, correct drape direction, sleeve length, border and pallu.
- When unsure, use the plain everyday garment and add a specific location.
- Ask a cultural reviewer from the community for any religious ceremony.
