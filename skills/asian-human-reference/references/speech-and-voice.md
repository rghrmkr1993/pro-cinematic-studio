# Speech and voice

Labels: [V] verified in a cited source (see sources.md), [E] estimate from general linguistic knowledge, [U] unverified. Accents are RANGES shaped by mother tongue, education, class and age. The goal is natural, respectful speech by native or fluent voices, never mockery.

## Rules for lipsync and voice tools
1. **Choose native-accent voices.** Pick a voice from a speaker of the same region and language, not an imitation by a non-native voice. If a tool has voice cloning, only use a voice the company has consent and a licence to use; never clone a real person without written consent.
2. **No accent as a joke.** No "Apu", no caricatured "very good, sir" call-centre stereotype, no mock Chinese or Japanese "r/l" speech, no exaggerated head-wobble paired with a comic voice.
3. **Match accent to character**, not to ethnicity: an Indian-origin US-born character speaks with an American accent; a Chennai professional may speak neutral-urban Indian English; a Singaporean may use Standard Singapore English at work and Singlish with friends.
4. **Let the language be the language.** If the story takes place in Hindi or Tamil, write the lines in that language (native-speaker review required) rather than English with an accent.
5. **Review by a native speaker** before publication, including subtitle spelling (diacritics, script).
6. **Pace**: Indian English and most Asian Englishes are often delivered at a steady pace with even syllables; do not rush or add "fast-talking" cues unless wanted.
7. **Test the model**: speech and lipsync quality for Tamil, Malayalam, Bengali and other languages is weaker in many models than English or Hindi [U]. Test a short clip before scaling and keep scripts short.
8. **Match mouth shapes**: retroflex and aspirated sounds, rounded vowels (Hindi "o"), and Japanese open vowels show different lip shapes; check sync for video models.

## Indian English: general traits [V where sourced]
- No single Indian English: English is shaped by 200+ mother tongues and varies by region and social background [V, OED, CIIL].
- **Retroflex consonants**: /t/ and /d/ are often retroflex (tongue curled back) instead of the alveolar sounds of British or American English; the "th" sounds are often realised as dental stops (so "thin" and "this" sound like "tin" and "dis" with a dental touch) [V, OED, arXiv 2212.09284].
- **v and w**: often merged into a single sound between them (/ʋ/), more so for speakers whose first language is Hindi or similar; the contrast is kept by many speakers of Standard Indian English, so it varies and is contested [V].
- **Rhythm**: tends to be syllable-timed (even beats), so unstressed vowels are not reduced as much as in American English [V, general source; U on strength].
- **Rhoticity**: sources disagree; many speakers pronounce /r/ more in urban and younger speech [V that sources disagree].
- **Aspiration**: voiceless stops (p, t, k) are often less aspirated [V].
- **Intonation**: stress on content words with a rising-falling pattern, lilting at phrase ends in some southern speakers; question tags such as "no?", "na", "isn't it?" used flexibly [E].

### By region [E, U unless noted]
- Hindi-belt and North: v/w merging, retroflex stops, words ending with a held vowel ("sir-ji"), Punjabi lilt with elongated vowels and emphatic stress.
- Tamil and other Dravidian speakers: voiced and voiceless consonants may be interchanged (for example b/p) as an influence from the first language [V, regional to the south]; a clear syllable beat; "-ing" often full.
- Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam: strong retroflexes and trilled r; Malayalam English may have rapid, rolling speech; Kannada English clear and even.
- Bengali: o/aa vowel shifts ("hospital" sounds like "hoshpital"), s/sh blends; soft intonation.
- Gujarati and Marathi: aspiration patterns differ [V for Gujarati and Tamil], dental sounds.
- Northeast: tonal influence in some speakers; Nagamese or Hindi may be the common language.
- Urban young professionals may have a neutral blend and an American-influenced accent after call-centre, university abroad or media exposure.

## Code-switching [E]
- Hinglish: Hindi and English mixed in one sentence, e.g. "Wo meeting kab hai? Send me the agenda, please." Switch at phrase boundaries, not randomly; Hindi verbs with English nouns ("update kar do", "check kar lo").
- Tanglish: Tamil and English, e.g. "Seri, I'll check and tell you da." Particles: "da/di" (informal, friends), "pa", "machan/machi" (close friends), "sollunga" (tell me, polite).
- Others: Manglish (Malaysian English) with "lah", "lor", "meh"; Singlish with "lah", "leh", "can", "aiyo"; Taglish (Tagalog and English) with "po" and "opo" for politeness; Benglish, Kanglish, Minglish, Spanglish equivalents are also real.
- Use particles in short doses (one or two per line). Over-sprinkling reads as mockery.

## Honorifics and address [E]
- India: "ji" (respect, suffix: Sharma-ji, Mataji), "sir" and "madam" or "ma'am" at work and with teachers, "bhai" (brother, also to male friends), "didi" or "akka" (elder sister, south and Bengal), "anna" (elder brother, in Tamil, Telugu and Kannada), "thambi" (younger brother), "uncle" and "aunty" for any older person, "saab", "babu", "amma", "appa", "chechi/chettan" (Kerala), "dada", "bhabhi".
- Sri Lanka: "aiya" (older brother), "akka", "malli". Bangladesh: "bhai", "apa" (sister), "bhaiya".
- East Asia: Japan "-san", "-sama", "-kun", "-chan", "sensei"; Korea "-ssi", "oppa", "unnie", "sunbae", "hyung", "noona", with levels of formal speech; China "lao" before surname, "xiao" for younger, "laoshi" (teacher).
- Southeast Asia: Thai "khun", "phi" (elder), "nong" (younger), "krub" (male polite ending), "ka" (female); Vietnamese kinship terms (anh, chi, em, bac); Filipino "po", "ate" (elder sister), "kuya" (elder brother); Indonesian "pak", "bu", "mas", "mbak"; Malay "cik", "encik", "kak", "abang".
- Do not use the wrong honorific for age or gender; check with a native reviewer.

## East Asian English [E, U unless noted]
- Japanese English: syllable-timed with added vowels between consonant clusters ("strike" becomes "su-to-rai-ku"), /l/ and /r/ merging, "th" as s or z [V that r/l is a known difficulty in sources]; polite hedging and downward intonation; rarely loud.
- Korean English: final consonants may be dropped or softened, /f/ and /p/ and /v/ and /b/ merging, flatter intonation; speakers vary widely, many with American-accented English after overseas stay.
- Mandarin and Cantonese English: tonal influence on stress and intonation, dropped final consonants, "th" as f or s, /l/ and /n/ merging in some regions; Hong Kong English has its own features.
- Do not mock these; keep subtitles with correct grammar unless a character's grammar is meaningfully part of the scene.

## Southeast Asian English [V general, E detail]
- Singapore English: Standard form in formal contexts and Colloquial Singapore English (Singlish) among friends; features such as missing auxiliary "be", particles lah/leh/lor, reduced consonant clusters [V from study notes, U on primary].
- Malaysian English: similar particles (lah, meh); mixing with Malay, Chinese languages and Tamil.
- Philippine English: /θ/ and /ð/ become t and d ("this" as "dis"), ship/sheep vowel confusion, "po" politeness, some articles dropped, local words ("high blood", "open the light") [V from study notes, U on primary]; many Filipinos speak near-native American-accented English.
- Thai English: tonal influence, final consonants dropped, polite ending particles (krub, ka); Vietnamese English: final consonants dropped; Indonesian English: syllable-timed, trilled r.

## Dialogue writing for natural phrasing
- Short, spoken lines (6 to 14 words) with contractions and pauses; no brochure language.
- Include fillers sparingly ("actually", "basically", "no?", "you know", "like").
- Indian English grammar: present continuous for habits ("I am having a headache"), "only" for emphasis, "itself", "kindly do the needful", "revert back" (common in workplace English); use only if the character is in that register and the scene is not a joke.
- Politeness shapes requests: indirect softeners ("if you don't mind", "could you please"), thanking elders with "thank you, ji" or in the local language.
- Names: use correct and varied names (not all "Raj" and "Priya"); South Indian names often include a village or father initial (K. Ravi), Sikh names Singh/Kaur, Bengali names Banerjee, Chatterjee; East Asian name order (family name first in China, Japan, Korea; keep consistent).
- Greeting and tone by language [E, U]:
  - Hindi: "namaste", "kaise hain aap?", warm and direct; "aap" (formal you), "tum" (familiar), "tu" (intimate or rude).
  - Tamil: "vanakkam", "epdi irukkeenga?" (how are you, polite), melodic, final particles "da/di/pa/la".
  - Telugu: "namaskaram", "ela unnaru?", soft, rhythmic with vowel endings.
  - Malayalam: "namaskaram", "sukhamano?", fast and rolling; "chetta/chechi" for elders.
  - Kannada: "namaskara", "hegiddira?", gentle, mild pace.
  - Bengali: "nomoshkar", "kemon achen?", lilting, expressive "ki bolchho!"
- Subtitles: write names with their correct spelling and provide English or Hindi subtitles reviewed by native speakers.

## Checks before delivery
- Voice sounds like a real person from the region (ask a native reviewer).
- No comedic accent or mock exaggeration.
- Honorifics fit age and relation.
- Code-switching is natural and not overdone.
- Lipsync matches the actual language.
- Voice licence and consent confirmed.
