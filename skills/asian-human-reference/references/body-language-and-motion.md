# Body language and motion

Labels: [E] craft estimate from observation and general cultural knowledge (no peer-reviewed source consulted), [U] unverified. Every item is a RANGE with exceptions. Use it to add believable specifics to video prompts, never as a rule for a nationality.

## Principles
- Behaviour depends on age, context, status, setting and personality far more than on region. A Mumbai investment banker, a Kerala farmer and a Delhi teenager move differently.
- Ask for one or two specific actions per shot; models fail on long action lists.
- Video models drift: lock the pose to a simple verb ("walks", "sits cross-legged") and avoid mixed gestures that cause limb merging. Check hands every shot (see `photoreal-humans`).
- Keep the cultural cue incidental, not the subject of the shot.

## Walking and gait [E]
- Child: bouncy, quick, hand held by an adult, trailing a school bag; often barefoot or in sandals at home.
- Youth: relaxed, phone in hand, headphones, in a group; loose swing of arms; urban youth in sneakers.
- Adult professional: purposeful but unhurried; bag over one shoulder, lanyard, phone call while walking; heels or formal shoes in offices.
- Elder: slower, shorter steps, may carry a walking stick, hand behind back or on a railing; pauses; hands on knees to stand.
- Street and market: weaving through crowds, shifting sideways, small apologetic hand gestures; carrying loads on head or in hands, women often balancing items with steady neck posture (common in rural contexts, not universal).
- Tradition: sarees or dhotis shorten stride; one hand may lift the hem on stairs; a mundu is folded up to knee when walking fast.
- Avoid stage-like marching, runway strut (unless a fashion shot) and exaggerated hip sway.

## Running [E]
- Child: chasing, uneven, laughing, arms wide; in school grounds.
- Adult: jogging in parks in the morning, groups of walkers; commuter sprinting for a train or bus in sarees or office wear with a bag clutched.
- Festival: children running with colour (Holi) or sparklers; sudden, scattered motion.
- Elder: rarely; brisk walk.

## Sitting [E]
- Floor sitting: cross-legged (sukhasana), on a mat, carpet or chair in temples, homes, and langars. Older people may sit with legs folded to the side or kneeling on heels (seiza in Japan) for tea and formal settings; women often tuck legs to one side in sarees. Knees up with arms around them is a casual pose among teenagers.
- Squatting (malasana) on heels: common in many Asian contexts for rest, waiting, bathing, cooking; people in some groups squat for long periods comfortably, with heels flat [E, U].
- Chairs: formal offices use upright posture, hands on lap or desk; at tea stalls people sit on benches with knees apart or on low stools.
- Children sit cross-legged in school assemblies, on mats in classrooms in some rural schools.
- Avoid pointing feet at people or shrines; keep sole angles neutral in temples.
- Seated working: people tuck one foot under, shift often, sit sideways at a desk.

## Eating with the hand [E]
- India: right hand (left hand not used for passing or eating in traditional settings); fingertips mix rice with curry into a small ball, pushed with the thumb to the mouth; the palm may touch the plate; no licking of fingers in formal meals; wash hands before and after; banana leaf meals in the south, thali with bowls (katori), roti torn with one hand and used as a scoop.
- Not everyone eats by hand; spoon-and-fork is common in urban India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh.
- Nepal, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh: similar right-hand eating; Southeast Asia: spoon and fork (spoon in right, fork pushes) in Thailand, Philippines; kamayan (eating with hands) in Philippines on banana leaf; Indonesia and Malaysia: right hand common at home; Vietnam, China, Korea, Japan: chopsticks (chopsticks must not stand upright in rice; do not pass food chopstick to chopstick; Korea uses a metal spoon and chopsticks; Japan holds bowls close to the mouth; noodles slurped).
- Eating posture: people lean slightly over the plate, elbows near the body; elders are served first in many families.
- Show real food, real mess, a wipe of hands; avoid hand-eating as exotic spectacle.

## Hand gestures [E, U]
- Namaste, pranam, wai, bow: see expressions file.
- Counting on finger joints (India), the "come here" palm-down scoop, the chin-point, the "wait" gesture (open hand tilted, palms facing the other person, often paired with "ek minute"), the "okay" fingers of cheek pinch (pinching the ear lobes for apology or promise), tapping chest, "tsk" with a mouth click.
- Avoid the thumbs-up, "OK" and V-sign as default positive signs without context; they carry different meanings in some places; the V-sign for photo poses is very common in East Asian photos.
- Photo poses: V-sign, cheek heart (Korea, Japan), peace sign; do not use as a stereotype but they do appear in real selfies.
- Hands emphasise speech in many Indian, Filipino and Chinese conversations; others are still; vary.

## Personal space and touch [E, U]
- Crowded daily life: people stand closer in queues, buses, trains and markets than many Western norms, with incidental contact; in formal conversations a respectful distance holds.
- Same-gender friends often hold hands, link arms or sit close in India and parts of Southeast Asia; this is not romantic by default.
- Opposite-gender public touching is more restrained in many places; handshakes may vary (many women and men greet with namaste instead; some religious people avoid cross-gender handshakes).
- Touching elders' feet, hand on head blessing, hugging at weddings: normal in context.
- Do not touch someone's head in Thai and other Buddhist contexts; avoid pointing feet.
- Shoes off at the home entrance; guests are offered water and tea first.

## Behaviour by age and role [E]
- Elder: seated at the head of the table or on the best chair, addressed first, listened to; may be active (market, gardening, temple walk, tai chi in Chinese parks, mahjong) and mischievous; not always frail or wise. Grandparents feed children by hand or tell stories.
- Child: playful, curious, shy with strangers, shows respect by greeting elders; school uniforms; carries heavy bags; not a beggar or poverty prop by default.
- Youth: phones, reels, cafes, gyms, college campuses, cricket or football on the street; fashion varies; do not show everyone as studious or a gamer.
- Professional: laptop, meetings, lanyards, whiteboards, formal-casual clothes; speaking style is polite and direct; a doctor, engineer, designer, chef, farmer, pilot, athlete; women in leadership roles and men in caring roles should appear naturally.
- Household and service work: show dignity, real uniforms, normal interaction; avoid servile postures or invisibility.
- Mother, father, siblings: family members share a table or couch; multigenerational homes are common but nuclear homes exist too.

## Micro-motion cues for video [E]
- Blink rate: roughly 15 to 20 blinks a minute at rest, fewer (about 3 to 8) when concentrating on a screen, more when nervous or speaking [U, general physiology]. Prompt "natural slow blinks, occasional double blink", never a fixed stare.
- Breathing: chest or shoulder rise about every 3 to 5 seconds at rest; deeper before speaking; visible in sleeveless or tight clothing; nostrils flare slightly during exertion.
- Weight shifts: shift from one foot to the other every few seconds while standing; lean on one hip, tuck a foot; adjust a sleeve, dupatta, glasses or hair.
- Fidgeting: touching face or neck, adjusting bangle or watch, tapping a finger, twisting a ring.
- Eye movement: saccades, brief glances away while thinking, glances at phone or other person; avoid a steady stare.
- Head: small nods, tilts while listening; micro tilts in speech.
- Speech: small head moves with stress; jaw and lips align with the voice; add pauses and breath.
- Cloth physics: sarees and dupattas move after the body (drag), mundu folds, long hair swings with a delay; jewellery sways and jingles lightly.
- Environment cues: sweat sheen in humid weather, hair strand stuck to the forehead, squinting in sun, steam from tea, dust in light.
- Camera: handheld micro sway for documentary feel; locked tripod with slow push for ads; avoid whip pans with faces that morph.
- Loops: for 5 to 10 second clips, ask for one action (turns, smiles, lifts the cup), a clear start and end pose.
- Check frames for skin tone drift, shrinking eyes, bindi or jewellery vanishing, and extra fingers.
