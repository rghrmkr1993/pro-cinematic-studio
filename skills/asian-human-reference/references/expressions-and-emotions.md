# Expressions and emotions

Labels: [V] verified in a cited source, [E] estimate/craft knowledge, [U] unverified.

## Principles
- FACS (Ekman and Friesen, 1978; revised 2002) describes muscle actions as Action Units (AU); it describes movement, not meaning [V]. The AU lists below follow the widely published EMFACS prototypes [V general]; AU numbers are in brackets.
- Human faces are not one template. Facial expression models differ by culture: in a 2012 PNAS study East Asian observers' models of emotions overlapped more and relied more on the eyes, while Western models relied more on the mouth [V, Jack et al. 2012]. A critique (Yan et al. 2015) found smaller differences [V]. Use this as a craft hint, not a rule: for restrained or formal scenes, put more signal in the eyes and brows and less in the mouth.
- Display rules (norms about showing emotion) vary by context, relationship, age and status, not by nationality alone. Indians, Japanese, Koreans, Thais and others all laugh loudly, cry openly and argue in private. Public restraint is a context, not a trait.
- Models over-smile and over-act. Ask for small, specific actions and neutral resting faces.
- Include asymmetry, micro-expressions and onset: real faces move in 200 to 500 ms for micro-reactions and 0.5 to 4 s for felt expressions [E].
- Eyelid shape changes how AU actions look: with a monolid or hooded lid, brow raise (AU1+2) shows less lid space, so describe it as "brows lift, forehead lines, lid stays smooth"; eye widening (AU5) shows as a rounder opening and visible lash line rather than a new crease [E]. Do not add a double lid to show surprise.
- Wrinkle visibility depends on age and skin. Keep age-appropriate lines.

## Emotions with intensity levels 1 to 3

Level 1 subtle (everyday), level 2 clear, level 3 strong (use rarely, usually private moments). Add a gaze line and a body line.

### Smile: social versus Duchenne
- Duchenne (genuine enjoyment) is AU6 + AU12; the mouth-only social smile is AU12 alone (often called a Pan-Am or polite smile) [V]. The marker is debated: AU6 also appears in posed smiles and may track intensity, not authenticity [V]. Use as craft only.
- 1: "polite social smile, mouth corners lift slightly, eyes unchanged, closed lips".
- 2: "warm smile, cheeks lift, small crow's feet, upper teeth show a little".
- 3: "broad delighted smile, cheeks pushed up, eyes narrowed, teeth fully visible, head tilts".
- Cultural: smiles can mask embarrassment or discomfort (Japan, Thailand's "land of smiles", India) as a polite device; show a tight smile with tense eyes when covering unease [E].

### Laughter
- AU6 + AU12 + AU25/26, with head tilt back; shoulders shake; eyes may close (AU43).
- 1: "quiet chuckle, breath out through nose, small smile". 2: "open laugh, head back slightly, shoulders move". 3: "doubled over, hand to chest or stomach, eyes shut, tears in the corners".
- Hand covering mouth while laughing is common in parts of East Asia, especially for women, but not universal [E].

### Anger
- AU4 + AU5 + AU7 + AU23 (+ 17/24); jaw set, nostrils flare (AU38).
- 1: "irritated, brows pulled slightly down, lips pressed, flat voice". 2: "frown, hard stare, jaw clenched, nostrils tight". 3: "furious, brows low, eyes wide and hard, lips pulled back over teeth, veins at the temple".
- Cultural: public anger is often controlled (low voice, tight jaw, cold politeness), loud anger more common in private or family disputes; do not make anyone "hot-tempered by default" [E].

### Sadness
- AU1 + AU4 + AU15 (+ 17); eyes glisten; lower lip trembles.
- 1: "quiet sadness, inner brows slightly raised, eyes lowered". 2: "mouth corners down, brows drawn, eyes wet". 3: "crying, face crumpled, chin trembles, tears running".
- Cultural: grief may be loud (wailing at some funerals in parts of India) or held in (stoic in public in East Asia); avoid making either a stereotype [E].

### Fear
- AU1 + AU2 + AU4 + AU5 + AU20 + AU25/26.
- 1: "wary, eyes slightly wide, breath held". 2: "brows raised and drawn, eyes wide, lips stretched". 3: "terror, wide eyes, mouth open, face pale, hands up".

### Surprise
- AU1 + AU2 + AU5 + AU26 (brief).
- 1: "mild surprise, brows lift". 2: "eyes wide, brows high, mouth slightly open". 3: "startled, jaw dropped, hand to chest, step back". Surprise lasts under a second before turning into another expression.

### Disgust
- AU9 + AU10 (+ AU15/16/17), upper lip raised.
- 1: "faint distaste, nose slightly wrinkled". 2: "nose wrinkled, upper lip raised, head turns away". 3: "revulsion, gag-like grimace, hand to mouth".
- Avoid disgust toward foods of a community (cow, beef, pork, fish, kimchi, durian, fermented items) as a joke at the expense of a culture.

### Contempt
- Unilateral AU12 + AU14 (one-sided lip corner tightening) [V in FACS descriptions; whether contempt is universal is debated, U].
- 1: "faint smirk on one side". 2: "one-sided sneer, eyes half-lidded". 3: "clear disdain, chin lifted, looking down the nose".

### Shyness
- Gaze down and away (aversion), small smile (AU12 + AU24), head tilt, touching hair or neck, hand over mouth, shoulders in.
- 1: "bashful, small smile, glances down". 2: "ducks head, covers smile with hand, soft laugh". 3: "flustered, turns away, hides face with dupatta or sleeve".
- Do not pair "shy" with "submissive woman". Men are shy too.

### Pride
- Chin lifted (AU53), small smile (AU12 light), chest open, steady gaze, hands on hips or folded behind.
- 1: "quiet pride, small closed-mouth smile, upright". 2: "chin up, eyes bright, standing tall". 3: "beaming, tears of pride, hand on chest" (parent at a graduation, athlete after a win).

### Affection
- Soft AU6 + AU12, slow blink, head tilt; touching the arm, forehead, or holding hands (same-gender hand-holding is common in friendship in India, so state friendship or couple).
- 1: "gentle look, soft smile". 2: "warm smile, eyes crinkle, leaning in". 3: "tender embrace, forehead to forehead, eyes closed".
- Public affection between couples is often restrained in many Asian settings [E]; family warmth (feeding a bite, touching feet, a hand on the head) carries more affection in scenes than kissing.

### Neutral and thinking
- Resting face with relaxed lips, small natural brow movement, slow blink; "listening with slight head nod".

## Culture-specific nonverbal cues

Treat as options with a context; they are common, not universal.

- **Indian head wobble (head bobble)**: side-to-side tilt of the head in arcs [V]. Meanings depend on speed, amplitude and context: slow and gentle often "yes, I agree"; light and quick "okay, I hear you" or "noted"; with raised brows can mean "maybe", "I understand but..."; sometimes a soft non-committal "yes" or a polite way to avoid a direct "no" [V, descriptive sources]. A clear "no" is a head shake, often subtler. More frequent in South India and Sri Lanka [U]. Prompt: "small relaxed head tilt side to side while listening, smile, eyes engaged". Do not caricature with a rubber-neck exaggeration or add it to every Indian character. Origin claims (Bharatanatyam) are unverified [U].
- **Nod and tilt**: In some regions a single upward chin lift (head toss back) means "what?" or "yes". [E, U]
- **Namaste/namaskar**: palms together at chest or chin height, small head dip, said "namaste" or "namaskaram" (south) or "vanakkam" (Tamil); used greeting to elders and in formal settings. Not used by everyone; handshake is also common in urban offices. Do not add it to every greeting. Sikh greeting: "Sat Sri Akal" with a folded hand; Muslim: "Assalamu alaikum" with a hand to chest or a handshake and embrace (e.g., Eid hug three times in some communities) [E].
- **Touching feet (pranam, charan sparsh)**: younger person bends and touches an elder's feet (or knees) then their own forehead or chest; elder blesses with a hand on the head ("khush raho", "aashirwad"). Common in many Hindu and some other families on greeting, festivals, exams, weddings; not universal. Do not show it as servile; it is respect and love, done briefly and casually in many homes [E].
- **Japanese bow (ojigi)**: about 15 degrees for casual greeting, 30 degrees for polite business use, 45 degrees or more for deep thanks or apology; straight back, hands at sides (men) or clasped in front (women, traditional) [V, travel/culture sources; sources differ on exact ranges]. Match the other person's depth. Shorter bows can look insincere. Nodding while talking and light bows on the phone are common (aizuchi nods).
- **Thai wai**: palms together, bow head; thumbs near chin for peers, near nose for elders or seniors; younger or lower status initiates; very senior people (monks) do not return it [V, travel/culture sources; hand heights simplified, U]. Do not wai for children or service staff in a way that suggests hierarchy for show.
- **Korean bow and two-handed giving**: shallow bow, hand on forearm when giving or receiving with one hand as a sign of respect; both hands for elders [E, U].
- **Chinese greeting**: nod or handshake in business, "ni hao" is used less among familiar people than outsiders think; offering tea with both hands, finger tap thank-you (tap two fingers on the table in Cantonese culture) [E, U].
- **Eye contact**: sustained direct eye contact toward elders or seniors can seem challenging in many Asian and Indian contexts; looking down or to the side shows respect, especially for the young and in hierarchical settings; peers and urban professionals look directly and comfortably [E, U]. Do not force "downcast eyes" for all women.
- **Public restraint**: lower volume, less touching between genders, small smiles in formal settings (Japan, Korea, parts of China); expressive, close and loud in families and festivals. Show both modes.
- **Pointing**: pointing with the chin or lips (India, Philippines lip point) and pointing with an open palm is more polite than a finger [E, U].
- **Beckoning**: palm down and fingers scoop, not palm up, in much of Asia [E, U].
- **Hand on heart** after greeting or when apologizing in Muslim and some South Asian contexts [E].
- **Head touch**: do not touch the head of an adult in a Thai or other Buddhist-majority setting; for children it is gentle blessing in India (hand on head by elder). [E, U]
- **Politeness phrases in face**: Southeast Asian "smile to cover" a mistake (Thai, Filipino, Indonesian senyum) can signal embarrassment, not amusement [E, U].
